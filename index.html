How a hand works

Everyone plays against the dealer, not against each other. Number cards count as their face value, face cards (J/Q/K) count as 10, and an Ace counts as either 1 or 11 — whichever helps the hand more. Getting closer to 21 than the dealer wins; going over 21 ("busting") loses immediately, even if the dealer busts too.

Each player gets two cards face up. The dealer gets one card face up (the "up-card") and one face down (the "hole card"). Players act first, choosing from:

HitTake another card
StandTake no more cards, end your turn
DoubleDouble your bet, take exactly one more card, then stand
SplitIf your two cards match, separate them into two hands, each with its own bet
SurrenderGive up the hand immediately for half your bet back (not offered everywhere)

Once every player is done, the dealer flips the hole card and plays by fixed rules — draw to 17 or more, no choices involved (whether the dealer hits a "soft 17" depends on the table, covered below). Whoever is closer to 21 without busting wins that hand; a tie is a "push" and the bet is simply returned.

Blackjack and the 3:2 vs 6:5 payout

A "natural" blackjack — an Ace with a 10-value card as your first two cards — beats any non-blackjack hand instantly and traditionally pays 3:2: a $10 bet wins $15. Many modern tables, especially single- and double-deck games, instead pay 6:5: that same $10 bet only wins $12.

That difference sounds small but isn't. Swapping 3:2 for 6:5 adds roughly 1.4% to the house edge — often more than doubling it — because blackjacks come up often enough (about once every 21 hands) for the payout cut to add up fast. It's one of the single biggest red flags to check for at a table, and most experienced players avoid 6:5 games outright regardless of how good the other rules look.

Spanish 21

Spanish 21 is played with a Spanish deck — a regular deck with all the 10s removed (48 cards instead of 52), leaving jacks, queens, and kings as the only 10-value cards. Removing the 10s alone would badly hurt the player, so Spanish 21 compensates with a stack of player-friendly rules: your blackjack always beats the dealer's (even a dealer blackjack, though not always in a tie), you can double after hitting and re-split most pairs, late surrender is standard, and a 5-or-more card hand totaling 21 (or certain 6-7-8 / 7-7-7 combinations) pays a bonus. Net result: the house edge ends up in a similar range to a good standard blackjack game, just reached by a completely different set of rules.

Other variants you might run into

Double Exposure: both dealer cards are dealt face up (huge player information advantage), balanced out by paying even money on blackjack instead of 3:2 and giving the dealer all ties (including 21-21).

Pontoon (UK/Australia): dealer cards are both face down, "Pontoon" replaces the term blackjack, "twist"/"stick" replace hit/stand, and a 5-card hand under 21 (a "five card trick") beats everything except a Pontoon.

Free Bet Blackjack: the casino gives you free double-downs and splits on qualifying hands (no extra risk to you), but claws it back by pushing — instead of losing — any dealer 22.

Double for less: a common house option (not really a separate variant) letting you double for any amount up to your original bet, not just an exact match — handy when you're short on chips, though full doubles are always better EV when you can afford them.

Whatever the variant, the same underlying idea from the Math tab applies: every rule change shifts the house edge a little, and correct strategy is just the rule-by-rule EV calculation worked out in advance.

Why basic strategy works

Blackjack looks like a card-luck game, but every decision is really a comparison of two known numbers: the expected value (EV) of hitting versus the EV of standing, given exactly which cards are left in the shoe. Basic strategy is simply the pre-computed answer — worked out long ago by computer simulation of billions of hands — to "which action loses the least money on average, for this hand, against this dealer up-card."

The house's edge doesn't come from better cards. It comes from turn order: you can bust and lose before the dealer even plays. Approximate bust probabilities by dealer up-card (6-deck, S17) look like this:

Chance dealer busts35%37%40%42%42%26%24%23%23%17%

Figures are commonly-cited approximations for illustration, not exact for every rule set.

That's why 4, 5, and 6 are called the dealer's "bust cards" — stand on your stiff hands (12–16) against them and let the dealer take the risk instead. Against a strong card like 10 or an Ace, the dealer rarely busts, so your stiff hand has to take its own chances by hitting, even though it also risks busting.

Take hard 16 vs dealer 10, the classic no-win spot: standing wins roughly 23% of the time long-run; hitting wins roughly 26%. Neither is good — you're picking the smaller loss, not a winner. That's the whole idea of basic strategy: it doesn't promise winning hands, it minimizes expected loss on the hands that are already unfavorable, and takes full advantage (doubling, splitting) on the hands that are favorable.

Where card counting fits in

Basic strategy assumes an average, unknown mix of cards left in the shoe. But the mix keeps changing as cards are removed. A shoe rich in tens and aces helps the player more than the dealer — you get more blackjacks (paid 3:2), the dealer busts more often on stiff hands, and doubling/splitting get better. A shoe rich in low cards helps the dealer.

Counting systems assign a small value to each rank so the running total tracks that balance. Hi-Lo (2–6 = +1, 7–9 = 0, 10–A = −1) is the standard teaching system. Dividing the running count by the decks remaining gives the true count — a rule-of-thumb says each true count point is worth roughly 0.5% of extra player edge in a 6-deck S17 game. At a true count of 0 the house edge is around 0.5%; at true count +4 the player edge is roughly +1.5%. These are rough, widely-cited approximations, not exact for every rule combination.

Other systems trade simplicity for extra accuracy: KO is unbalanced (no true-count division needed, simpler at the table but less precise), Hi-Opt I and Omega II weight certain ranks more heavily (Omega II is a "level 2" system, meaning card values go up to ±2) for a stronger correlation with actual edge, at the cost of being harder to track.

Turning an edge into money: bet sizing

Knowing your edge is only useful if your bet size responds to it. The standard approach borrows from the Kelly criterion, which says the bankroll fraction to bet is roughly:

f* = edge ÷ variance

Blackjack's variance per hand is around 1.3 (it swings more than a coin flip because of blackjacks, doubles, and splits). So at a true count of +4, with a ~1.5% edge, Kelly says bet about 1.5 ÷ 130 ≈ 1.2% of your bankroll. Most card counters bet a fraction of that ("half-Kelly" or less) to reduce the risk of a bad losing streak wiping them out, since full Kelly is mathematically optimal for long-run growth but rough in the short run.

Beyond basic strategy

Serious counters also learn a short list of index plays (sometimes called the "Illustrious 18") — specific hands, like taking insurance or standing on 16 vs 10, where the correct action flips once the true count crosses a threshold. This trainer doesn't drill those, since they depend on the exact rules and count system in play, but the concept follows directly from the same EV logic above: as the count shifts, the deck's makeup shifts, and every so often that's enough to flip the max-EV action.

Note: card counting is not illegal, but casinos can — and do — ask suspected counters to stop playing blackjack or leave. Use this as a way to sharpen a real mental skill, not as a guarantee of a night's winnings; even with a real edge, variance means any single session can still lose money.
